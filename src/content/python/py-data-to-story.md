A chart is not the end of an analysis — it is how the analysis reaches someone who was not there for the work. This lesson is about that last step: turning a result into something a reader actually takes away.

You will learn:

- picking the one message a chart or report should carry
- structuring a short analysis around it
- writing a title that states the takeaway, not just the topic
- annotating the evidence, and writing captions
- reviewing for misleading choices before sharing

## Pick the one message

A real analysis usually turns up several findings. Trying to show all of them in one chart (or one slide) usually means none of them land clearly. Before building the final version, ask: if a reader remembers exactly one sentence from this, what should it be? Everything else in the chart should be in service of that one sentence, not competing with it for attention.

## Structure a short analysis

A workable shape for a short write-up: the question, the one-sentence answer, the evidence (a chart or two, each doing one job), and any important caveat. Leading with the answer (rather than building up to it at the end) respects a reader who may only have time for the first paragraph.

## Title as the takeaway

- Descriptive: "Average delay by carrier, 2013."
- Takeaway: "Airline X had the worst average delay of any major carrier in 2013."

The second version tells a skimming reader the actual point, even if they never look closely at the chart itself. A descriptive title is not wrong, exactly — it is simply a weaker use of the one line of text every reader is guaranteed to read.

## Annotating the evidence

If the claim is "this carrier is the worst", the chart should make it effortless to see *which bar that is* — a highlighted colour, a direct label, or an arrow pointing at it, rather than expecting the reader to cross-reference a legend and find the tallest bar themselves.

## Writing captions

A caption is where a chart's supporting details belong: the data source and time period, a caveat about what was excluded (cancelled flights, an outlier removed, a small sample size), or the precise number behind a rounded label on the chart itself. This keeps the visual clean while still giving a careful reader everything they need to judge it.

## Reviewing for misleading choices

Before sharing a finished chart, it is worth deliberately checking it against the honesty concerns from earlier in this section: does the axis start at zero (or is there a good, stated reason it does not)? Is the comparison being made a fair one (same time period, same population, same units)? Would the same chart, shown to someone who disagreed with the conclusion, still look fair to them? This is a different question from "is the underlying data correct" — a chart built from entirely accurate numbers can still leave a misleading impression through framing alone.

## Delivering as a notebook or slide

A notebook (with the analysis code visible alongside the findings) suits a technical audience who might want to check or extend the work themselves. A slide or a short written report suits a decision-making audience who mainly need the conclusion and enough evidence to trust it. The same underlying analysis, delivered either way, should still lead with the one message it exists to communicate.

## Common mistakes

- Cramming every finding into one chart instead of choosing the single most important one.
- A title that describes the chart's contents instead of stating its conclusion.
- A caption that repeats the chart instead of adding context the visual could not carry.
- Skipping a final honesty review because the numbers themselves are already known to be correct.

## Recap

- Choose the one message before building the final chart; let everything else serve it.
- A takeaway title states the conclusion; a caption carries the supporting details and caveats.
- Annotate the specific evidence for a claim directly, rather than leaving a reader to find it.
- Review the finished chart for framing choices that could mislead, separately from checking the numbers are correct.
